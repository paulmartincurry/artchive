# The Artchive — project brief

Paul's site: iconic art and short, powerful writing for creative people.
Live at https://paulmartincurry.github.io/artchive/ (GitHub Pages, deploys from `main`).

## What it is

- **Audience:** starts as Paul's own tool, then creative friends, then any creative person. Also a portfolio piece. Build for strangers.
- **The job:** spark an idea and hand over raw material. Get in, get hit by something, go make work. Lingering is a bonus, not the goal.
- **Art leads.** It is a visual experience first. Writing takes less room but gets equal care. Art-and-text pairings are a future experiment, not the spine.
- **Point of view:** drama, intensity, life, poetry. A personal canon with a bias, not a neutral survey.
- **Famous first, deep cuts inside.** The works people will search for must be there, or it fails the public test. Over time, lean further into the overlooked.
- **Scale:** tens of thousands eventually, behind a curated front of house.

## Rules for content

1. **Real texts only.** Nothing presented as the work is written by an AI. No paraphrases, no retellings, no invented titles. AI chooses and tags; it never authors the work.
   - **The note's voice (Paul, 15 Nov 2026): a museum run by people who make things.** He wants it to sound like a museum with just enough of a person in it to read as a curator or fellow creative, never cold, and never forced AI conversational, which he says would ruin the project. The voice comes from what the curator notices, not from tone: how old the maker was, how long it took, what it was made with, who paid, what went wrong, what happened to it afterwards. Full plain sentences, two to five of them; several facts when a work has several; one sentence when it has one; nothing but the label when there is no good fact. His own example: "The painting was made straight onto the plaster of his dining room wall. Francisco de Goya was in his seventies when he painted it, and he never exhibited it." Never what a work means or makes people feel; no praise words; vary how cards are built so they do not read as a template; "death", not "passing". Every fact must come from a fetched source, with the supporting snippet kept (`tools/grow_kit.py` `save_line()`, brief in `out/BRIEF8.md`); notes in this voice carry `noteRev: 4` and `noteSource`. Paul approved the voice after a 52-work pilot (15 Nov 2026) and said do it all. The rollout is done (16 Nov 2026): 1,711 of 1,719 notes carry it (`noteRev: 4`). Wave 1 (438 artworks) used trimmed Wikipedia wikitext fetched with `index.php?title=…&action=raw` (about one a second from the sandbox, far faster than the REST API), brief in `out/BRIEF9.md`. Wave 2 (the other 494 artworks and all 752 writing pieces) used the museum's own record from the Met, Chicago and Cleveland APIs plus the Wikipedia articles on the work and the maker, brief in `out/BRIEF10.md`; for writing, many files held only the author's article, so those notes are about the writer at the time of the piece, not the piece. Evidence and the replaced notes are in `data/wave1-notes-*.json` and `data/wave2-notes-*.json`. Five works are label only (`labelOnly: true`, empty note) because their record held no fact. Eight keep the old, unsourced note because no source about the object itself was found: VAULT-128, 275, 325, 328, 331, 484, LIT-447, LIT-130. Where a source has no concrete fact, the work gets the label alone. Above the note sits the real museum label (title, maker, date, medium, holder); size is shown on the label where the holder states it (17 Nov 2026).
   - **The one exception is the context note** (`note`). Claude writes these. (The rest of this paragraph is the older standard, kept for history; the voice above replaces it.) Paul credits that elsewhere on the site, so no per-note label. A note is a museum card with a bit more history or a bit about the maker, in a plain, friendly voice. Paul's instruction: keep it factual, don't get cute or clever. Facts only, no interpretation or mood-setting, no punchlines or asides to the reader, two to four short sentences, nothing that can't be checked against the museum record or the cited edition. If a fact is shaky, leave it out. Never quote the work or anyone else in a note.
2. **Text comes from sources**, not memory: Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, PoetryDB, museum collection APIs. Record where each piece came from.
3. **The pull quote is the unit.** Every writing piece has a short, strong excerpt (`hook`) that works on its own. For many visitors that is the whole point. The longer passage (`fullText`) is optional depth, one tap away. Never lose the short form, and the quote must be the source's exact words.
4. **Lead with the modern, respectful, agreed-upon wording** (Paul, 13 Nov 2026). For a translated piece the pull quote is one or two lines from the current standard translation, credited to translator and book (`audit: quoted`, with `license`). Where the old version is the agreed English (King James Bible, FitzGerald, an author's own English), or a translation is already modern and openly offered (A. S. Kline), keep it. Never show reporter-written dialect or an outdated name when a published standard version exists. The wording must be confirmed from fetched text of the book or a page that credits the translator: never from memory, and Claude does not translate.
   - **Keep the older passage as depth.** A public-domain translation of the longer passage goes in `olderText` with `olderTranslator`; the reader shows it behind "Longer passage". Full texts stay public domain or openly offered; only the one-or-two-line quote comes from a book in copyright.
   - If no modern wording can be confirmed, keep the public-domain text and say so in AUDIT.md. Cut only when no real text exists at all.
5. **Don't force balance.** Tag a piece for what it is. Fix thin shelves by adding pieces, not by re-labelling.

## Design principles

- Minimal, dark, quiet. Show the work, hide the UI. Less is more everywhere.
- One big search bar. Search returns the works themselves, not an organising layer.
- Captions off by default on the Wall; title on hover.
- Maker tools (plate, stencil, wheatpaste, duotone, broadside, loupe) are gone from the interface (Paul, 15 Nov 2026: "delete all of ours, we will brainstorm this in the future"). The buttons were removed, `setLens()` is pinned to the original image and scroll-to-zoom is off. The tool code is still in `index.html`, unreachable; strip it in its own careful pass or rebuild from it when Paul reopens the idea. Project stacks, the palette and the full-size link are not maker tools and stay.
- **What the site is, from Paul's answers on 15 Nov 2026.** Search first, surprise as a bonus. It looks like an art site and is secretly equal parts writing. Taste, in order: the dramatic and intense, then the beautiful and quiet, then the famous; the strange and overlooked comes last, as deep cuts, not front of house. No voice in words: his taste shows only in the curation. People find a thing, save it, and keep looking, so collections (boards, a download pile) are wanted. It is for him, his creative friends and any creative person at once. It works if he uses it weekly and strangers arrive. Free, meant to open doors. No links to his other projects for now. What would embarrass him: a wrong fact or fake quote, the site being slow, the site feeling AI-made. The shelf he reaches for that is missing: film stills.

## How it's built

- `index.html` — the whole app (CSS + JS inline). Edit in place; never regenerate.
- `data/works.js` — the library: 1,718 artworks + 922 writing pieces, one JSON array (17 Nov 2026, round 10 in progress).
- **Design batch (16 Nov 2026).** 56 objects and drawings from Cooper Hewitt (CC0, via `si()`), chosen by eye from about 700 candidates made before 1931: fraktur and lettering, Japanese katagami stencils, wallpapers and textiles (Walter Crane, Dresser, American Art Nouveau sidewalls), samplers, book covers, Dresser ceramics and metalwork, Tiffany glass, Grueby tiles, ornament prints, Bibiena and Basoli stage designs, theatre ceilings, Savoyard firework transparencies, clock and chair designs, Guimard. They carry `grow: 'steal'`. Posters that sell a product were left out (Paul: no ads). Album covers whose image is only the marbled binding were dropped: the image must show what the title names. Cooper Hewitt records are thin, so many notes are one or two sentences. The Smithsonian's image server (ids.si.edu), like Chicago's, serves the sandbox's headless browser a verification page, so check those images with curl or in Paul's Chrome.
- **More sources (16 Nov 2026).** `tools/sources.py` reaches five more collections and returns one record shape (title, maker, date, medium, size, holder, credit, description, provenance, image, rights, and `open` only when the holder marks the image free to reuse): the National Gallery of Art in Washington (`nga()`, from its GitHub open-data CSVs, cached in ~/src/nga; `openaccess=1` is CC0), the Rijksmuseum (`rijks()`, Linked Art API, no key; titles often only in Dutch or German, so keep the holder's title and note the Dutch one in `auditNote`), the V&A (`vam()`, images under the V&A's own non-commercial terms, so `open` is False), and the Library of Congress (`loc()`, open only when marked No known restrictions). The Met, Chicago and Cleveland are in ~/grow/harvest.py. The Smithsonian (`si()`, every Smithsonian museum including Cooper Hewitt, the Freer and Sackler and American Art; CC0 images marked open) uses Paul's api.data.gov key, kept in `~/.config/artchive/si_key` or `$SI_API_KEY` and never committed: the repo is public. If a new session lacks the file, ask Paul for the key again rather than looking for it in the repo or the chat. Paul's Biodiversity Heritage Library key and NYPL Digital Collections token (16 Nov 2026) sit beside it as `bhl_key` and `nypl_token`, under the same rule. Also public domain and not yet wired: Paris Musées, Nationalmuseum Stockholm, Yale, LACMA, the Getty, Europeana.
- **Film stills (16 Nov 2026).** The shelf Paul said was missing. 33 frames from 22 silent films released 1902 to 1929 (public domain in the United States), grabbed with ffmpeg from Internet Archive copies and hosted in `images/film/`. They carry `grow: 'film'`, `noHolder`, a `rights` line, and the medium `Photograph & Film`. A still is titled with the film's title and the frame's timecode in that copy (Nosferatu, frame at 1:00:36), which is a real reference, not an invented caption; `auditNote` names the copy. Never use an upload whose files say bluray, bdrip, remux or YTS, or a later re-edit (Moroder's Metropolis, Chaplin's 1942 Gold Rush). Choose frames from a contact sheet by eye; when a sharper copy is found, match the frame by image correlation and look at the match. Sunrise has a recorded score, so it is not labelled silent. A new medium, `Photograph & Film`, holds the stills and the 64 photographs that had been filed under Scroll & Paper, Print or Sculpture.
- **A page and a link for every work (16 Nov 2026).** `tools/build_pages.py` gives each work a `slug` (stored in works.js and never changed once set, so shared links keep working) and writes `w/<slug>.html`: image, label and note for art, pull quote, credit and note for writing (never the full text, so writing in copyright stays at one line), with Open Graph tags so a shared link previews. It also writes `sitemap.xml` and `robots.txt`, and stamps a version on the `data/works.js` script tag in index.html (GitHub Pages caches files for ten minutes, and without the stamp a returning visitor got new code with the old library). Run it after any change to works.js. In the app, an open work puts `#w/<id>` in the address bar, a `#w/<id>` link opens that work, and the lightbox's "Copy link" button copies the `w/<slug>.html` page. `openLightbox` and `closeLightbox` are wrapped at the end of the script for this; anything that needs to run on open or close should call them by name, not keep a reference to the original.
- **Speed (16 Nov 2026).** Grid tiles use `setTileImage()`: a `srcset` with Commons' 500px step or Chicago's 400px IIIF size, falling back to the full file if the small one fails. Unloaded tiles hold a 4:5 placeholder so lazy loading works (before, every tile in a batch had zero height and all 56 loaded at once). Cleveland images use the museum's `_web.jpg` (the `_print.jpg` files were 2.5 to 5 MB; they are kept as `highResUrl`), and Met images use the Met's `web-large` file instead of the full IIIF. First screen on a phone went from 6.2 MB of images to 1.1 MB; desktop from 8.8 to 4.6. **Slim library (18 Nov 2026):** the site no longer loads `data/works.js`. `tools/build_pages.py` writes `data/site.js`, a copy without the provenance fields the app never reads (noteSource, noteRev, isSeed, craftSpec, grow, license, mediumUnchecked, olderAuditNote, and auditNote unless the work is `attribution` or `unconfirmed`) and without literature fields that equal what the app rebuilds on load (quote, quoteAuthor, highResUrl, excerpt, pocketNo; the rebuild is right after `SEED_WORKS` in index.html). 5.4 MB to 3.9 MB raw, 1.2 MB to 1.0 MB gzipped. `data/works.js` stays the full master record that every tool edits; run build_pages after any change or the site will not see it. Chicago's image server (Cloudflare) refuses the sandbox's headless browser, so Chicago images can only be checked with curl or from Paul's browser.
- **Front of house (18 Nov 2026).** The Wall opens on a daily draw from a hand-picked pool in `frontOfHouse()` (index.html, just after `SEED_WORKS`): about 50 dramatic works, 25 quiet, 24 famous and 25 lines of writing, drawn 16, 6, 5 and 5 each day and woven so the first screen is mostly drama with writing between. The date seeds the draw, so everyone sees the same front on a given day; Shuffle still reshuffles. Change the pool by editing its id lists; pick works whose image reads at tile size (a Goya plate in a wide white mount reads less well than a painting).
- **Boards and the download pile (18 Nov 2026).** Saving and boards already existed as "stacks"; the interface now calls them boards, a new visitor starts with none, and the CSS and JSON exports are hidden. The Saved view has Tear sheet (one page of every saved work with label, rights line and a link to the full-size file or the source) and Download pile: a .zip, built in the browser by a small store-only ZIP writer (`makeZip`), of the free-to-use files in the current board with a credits.txt (title, maker, date, medium, size, holder, rights and photo credit, source link). It asks for Commons' 2560 px rendering, then the holder's file, then the screen image, and skips anything over 80 MB. Holders that refuse cross-origin fetches (on 18 Nov from Paul's Chrome: Cleveland's CDN, BHL, NASA's asset server, LACMA, the Met's API host) go into credits.txt as links; the Met's image server, Chicago, LoC, NGA, Smithsonian, Rijksmuseum, SMK, Getty, NYPL, Wellcome, ESO and the site's own files all hand files over. Look-and-learn works are named in credits.txt and never included.
- **Growth pass (15 Nov 2026).** Paul's brief: all art in its broadest sense; no advertising; the most-searched names plus who a creative audience comes looking for. **The line on dark subjects (Paul, 15 Nov 2026):** death in art is welcome and important. Skulls, vanitas, the dance of death, funerary art, war, the Isle of the Dead, Van Gogh's skull: all in, and he loves a lot of it. What stays out is material he does not see as art: medical and specimen photography, and sideshow material. Do not cut a work for being about death. Seven directions were run at about 100 pieces each (AUDIT.md, "Growth pass"): depth on the best-known artists and writers, design material to steal from, photography, the early twentieth century, words that are not poems (letters, manifestos, diaries, notebooks), the thin Love, Earth and Fury shelves, and visionary and mythic work. New artworks carry `grow` (heroes, famous, steal, weird, photo, c20). How it was done, for next time: artworks come from the Met (`/public/collection/v1.1/search`, the old search was retired), Chicago and Cleveland open-access APIs, which give a verified record and a rights flag in one call, or from Wikidata in bulk over SPARQL for famous works held elsewhere (Wikidata's dates and holders were wrong for about one in five and were corrected by hand, so never take them unread). New writing goes through `tools/grow_kit.py` `save_new()`, which refuses any text that does not match its fetched source line by line. Cards and notes for the new art were written by parallel agents from the museum record plus well-documented facts; those notes are not second-sourced. Keep each future batch to a fixed list with a finish line.
- **Rights: two tiers (Paul, 16 Nov 2026).** The site is for learning as much as for taking, so it carries two kinds of work. Every artwork has `rightsTier`:
  - `free`: free to take. Public domain in the United States (published before 1931, or US federal government work of any date, or a US work whose copyright was not renewed, checked one by one), or released by its holder under CC0. Full-size link shown; the lightbox says it is free to use. This is the raw-material tier: screen print it, cut it up.
  - `look`: look and learn. Still in copyright, shown for study and commentary under fair use, the model WikiArt and Wikipedia use. Paul's call on 16 Nov 2026: no cap per artist, curated to the works people come looking for; images screen-sized (up to about 1,000 px on the long side), never the full-resolution file; no full-size link, no export, no download control; a clear look-and-learn line in the lightbox; a sourced note on every one, because the commentary is what makes it fair use. These carry `fairUse: true`, `rights` and, where known, `imageW`/`imageH`, and are hosted in `images/fairuse/`. Take any down at once if a rights holder asks. If the site ever makes money, this tier is reviewed first. Claude flagged to Paul that a missing download button does not stop downloading and that larger copies weaken a fair-use claim; he accepted that risk.
  - **Assigned 16 Nov 2026.** Every artwork now carries `rightsTier` (17 Nov 2026: 1,502 `free`, 133 `look`; 151 carry `photoCredit`). The licence of every Wikimedia Commons file (518) was read from its file page. A photograph of a public-domain object (sculpture, building, manuscript) usually carries the photographer's own CC licence, so 126 free works also carry `photoCredit` ("name, CC BY-SA 2.0") and the lightbox and page say "Free to use with credit. Photo: …". Museum CC0 images, LoC, NGA, SMK, Rijksmuseum and the film stills need no credit. Warhol's Soup Cans (Commons calls it never renewed, but the estate licenses it) and Rivera's 1934 mural (a photographer's CC photo of a work still in copyright) were moved to `look`. A new piece needs `rightsTier`, and `photoCredit` when the image's licence asks for attribution.
  - **First additions under the new rules (16 Nov 2026):** 30 WPA posters (VAULT-1070 to 1099; Federal Theatre, National Park Service, health and wartime posters, 1936 to 1943) and 20 FSA/OWI photographs (VAULT-1100 to 1119: Evans, Lange, Rothstein, Parks, Lee, Wolcott, Shahn, Delano, Bubley, Vachon, two Kodachromes from Pie Town). All from the Library of Congress, made by or for US federal agencies, marked No known restrictions, `grow` 'gov' and 'photo'. Images are the LoC's 1,024 px JPEGs on tile.loc.gov (cdn.loc.gov redirects there; use tile directly), from the original negative where the record offers one. Long LoC captions are shortened for the title, with the full caption kept in `auditNote`. `tools/sources.py` `loc()` finds them; `/pictures/item/<id>/?fo=json` gives the record. Skip hole-punched (killed) FSA negatives, and posters that sell a product or a town.
  - Writing in copyright is unchanged: one sentence of prose under fifteen words, confirmed in a scan, and never a line of a poem still in copyright (a limit Claude keeps whatever the site's rules).
  - Before Paul's 16 Nov decision, ten works (Dalí, Kahlo, Magritte, Pollock, Rothko, Basquiat, Lichtenstein, Escher, Banksy, Guernica) were the only fair-use works, one per artist at Wikipedia's low resolution. Early public-domain works by Picasso, Chagall, Miró, Duchamp, Brâncuși and O'Keeffe stay in the free tier.
- **Eras.** A fifth era, `1920–Now`, was added to the filter and to `classifyEraBucket` on 16 Nov 2026; 148 works dated after 1920 that had been filed under 1800–1920 were moved.
- Writing pieces carry `shelf` (Love, God, Earth, Death, War, Art), `emotionShort` (mood), `audit` (verified / loose / modern-translation / unconfirmed / attribution / quoted) and `auditNote`.
- Pieces with a modern pull quote carry `olderText`, `olderTranslator`, `olderSourceUrl` and `olderAuditNote` for the public-domain passage behind it. Re-sourced pieces also carry `translator` (shown in the reader's credit line), `license` (only when the text is not public domain) and a `sourceUrl` pointing at the edition the text was taken from; `auditNote` names the edition.
- Sourcing from the work sandbox: Project Gutenberg, archive.org and poetryintranslation.com can be fetched directly; Wikisource refuses. Standard Ebooks also works. Gutenberg's search page is unreliable from the sandbox; archive.org's `advancedsearch.php` is the dependable way to find an edition. Archive.org scans are OCR, so check them against a second scan and say in `auditNote` when errors were corrected by eye.
- Reading Room: a book is one shelf in one mood. Titles are in `RR_BOOK_TITLES` in `index.html`. A mood with fewer than four pieces on a shelf joins the nearest mood's book on that shelf (`RR_NEAREST`); the pieces keep their own tags. There are no catch-all books.
- Artwork images: 372 come from Wikimedia Commons. `imageUrl` is the 960px thumbnail and `highResUrl` the original file (some originals are hundreds of MB, so never put an original in `imageUrl`). All 372 files were confirmed to exist on 12 Nov 2026 via the Commons API from Paul's browser; the sandbox itself cannot reach Wikimedia. 60 images narrower than 960px use the original.
- Artworks carry `audit` and `auditNote` too (13 Nov 2026). `verified` means the title, maker and date were checked against the museum's own record (the 129 Met, Chicago, Cleveland and SMK works, which also had their medium checked) or against the Wikipedia and Wikidata records for that work. `unconfirmed` means the source is a general article or nothing was checked; the lightbox says "details not yet verified" on those. `museum` is the real holder or empty: never a placeholder. Where a medium could not be checked it was moved to `mediumUnchecked` and `medium` left empty or filled from Wikidata. Anonymous works are credited "Maker unknown", not to an invented workshop. The site no longer claims CC0 or public domain for anything.
- Wikipedia and Wikidata rate-limit the sandbox hard. Use the batch APIs (`action=query` with 20 titles, `wbgetentities` with 45 ids), a descriptive User-Agent, and pauses.
- Artworks no longer carry a stored `quote`. The lightbox can still pair an artwork with a line from the writing library on click (`matchCanonicalVerse`), which draws on audited pieces.
- Search: an exact phrase in a title, artist, pull quote or full text always outranks loose word matches.
- The app de-duplicates on title + artist with anything in parentheses stripped, so two pieces by one author need titles that differ outside the brackets.
- `AUDIT.md` and `audit.csv` — record of the November 2026 audit of the writing.
- **Every writing piece is tied to an edition** (13 Nov 2026): its `auditNote` names the edition its text was taken from or checked against, or it is a short credited quote (`audit: quoted`). The one exception is LIT-058. The first audit's `verified` meant only that the wording looked like a published text; a line-by-line check found about two in three of those were reworded or stitched, and they were replaced. Keep to the method for anything new: strip piece and edition to letters only, split the piece into sentences and lines, and require every one to occur in the edition; pull text from the edition between a start and an end phrase, never retype it; mark a skipped stretch inside a quote with an ellipsis. Archive.org's full-text search (`/services/search/beta/page_production/?service_backend=fts&user_query="phrase"`) returns snippets even from lending-only books and is the way to confirm a modern translation's wording.
- Wikimedia from the sandbox: `https://en.wikipedia.org/api/rest_v1/page/summary/<Title>` (lead image, Wikidata id) and `https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Special:EntityData/<Q>.json` work at about one call every three seconds; `w/api.php` is refused. 960px thumbnails on upload.wikimedia.org load but throttle, so retry after 20 seconds. Bulk lookups (hundreds of works) are too slow this way.
