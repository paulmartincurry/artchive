When the friend shows his inmost heart to his friend; the lover to his best beloved; when man does not vainly shrink from the eye of his Creator, loathsomely treasuring up the secret of his sin; then deem me a monster, for the symbol beneath which I have lived, and die! I look around me, and, lo! on every visage a Black Veil!

The Minister's Black Veil — On every visage a Black Veil

Nathaniel Hawthorne · 1836

Hawthorne may have taken the idea from a real clergyman, Joseph Moody of York, Maine, who accidentally killed a friend as a young man and wore a black veil from the funeral until his own death. The story came out without Hawthorne's name in The Token and Atlantic Souvenir for 1836. In the spring of 1837 Horatio Bridge offered to cover the risk of gathering his magazine stories into Twice-Told Tales.
