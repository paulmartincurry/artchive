Who wrongs thee, Sappho? For even if she flies she shall soon follow, and if she rejects gifts shall yet give, and if she loves not shall soon love, however loth.

Ode to Aphrodite (Fragment 1)

Sappho of Lesbos · ca. 600 BCE

Translated by Henry Thornton Wharton

The poem survives almost whole because Dionysius of Halicarnassus quoted all of it in his treatise On Composition, as an example of smooth writing. Part of it is also on a second-century papyrus found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt. It is one of only four surviving works in which Sappho names herself. That the beloved is a woman rests on a single feminine word in line 24, a reading first proposed by Theodor Bergk in 1835 and not fully accepted until the 1960s.
